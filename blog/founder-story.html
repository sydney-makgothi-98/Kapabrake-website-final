Built to Stop Things: The Founder's Vision Behind Kapabrake

By the Kapabrake Team January 2024 7 min read

There's a particular silence that follows a mine hoist failure. The machinery stops, the men underground wait, and everyone above surface holds their breath. It's a silence that the founders of Kapabrake heard once — and decided they never wanted to hear again.

This is not a story about technology. It's a story about purpose. About three engineers who looked at an industry they'd spent their careers in, saw something fundamentally broken, and decided to fix it.

The Incident That Changed Everything

In the mid-1990s, the founders were working as maintenance engineers on a deep-level gold mine in the Witwatersrand. It was the kind of environment that tested men and machines equally — dust so thick it coated everything, heat that came up from below in waves, and machinery that never stopped.

Then a hoist brake failed.

No one was killed. The emergency systems — other brakes, designed exactly for this scenario — held. But the investigation revealed something troubling: the failed brake had degraded slowly, predictably, in ways that a better-designed system would have flagged months earlier. The failure was not sudden. It was the result of a component that was never suited to the environment it was working in.

"We had imported brakes that were designed for European conditions. They were good brakes — for Europe. But this was not Europe. This was a South African gold mine, and the designers had never seen one."

The mine recovered. Production resumed. But the three engineers — we'll call them the Founding Team — had been changed by the experience. They began to ask a question that would consume the next several years of their lives: what would a brake system look like if it were designed specifically for African mining, from the ground up?

Five Years of Nights and Weekends

They didn't quit their jobs. Not at first. What they did was spend every evening, every weekend, every vacation day working on the answer to that question.

They read everything. They visited workshops. They spoke to every brake manufacturer they could find and asked the questions that manufacturers rarely wanted to answer: what fails first? Under what conditions? How quickly does performance degrade in high-dust, high-heat environments? What does failure actually look like from the inside?

They built prototypes in a garage in Krugersdorp. Then they destroyed them — deliberately, methodically — to understand their failure modes. They developed their own test rigs because the ones that existed weren't representative of African mining conditions. They kept notes, adjusted, rebuilt, destroyed again.

By 1998, they had something. Not a product — not yet. But a design philosophy. A set of engineering principles that would eventually become the foundation of every Kapabrake product ever manufactured.

The Core Principles

The founding team's design philosophy can be summarised in three principles that remain unchanged at Kapabrake today:

Design for failure. Don't design a brake for how it will work when everything is fine. Design it for how it will behave when something goes wrong. Every failure mode must have a safe response — not a catastrophic one.

Design for the actual environment. Not a theoretical environment. Not a laboratory environment. The actual environment where the system will operate, with its dust, its heat, its vibration, its unpredictable operators and imperfect maintenance schedules.

Test beyond limits. If the specification requires performance at 80°C, test to 110°C. If the duty cycle requires 10,000 stops per year, test to 15,000. The field will surprise you. Never let it surprise your brake.

Founding Kapabrake

Kapabrake was formally registered in 1999. The founding team left their employer positions, pooled their savings, leased a small workshop in the Johannesburg industrial belt, and hired two additional engineers and three machinists.

Their first product was a disc brake system for mine hoist applications rated to South African mine regulations — designed specifically for the temperature ranges, dust classifications, and load cycles typical of Witwatersrand deep-level mines. It was not the cheapest brake on the market. It was, by design, never going to be.

"We could have made a cheaper product," one of the founders has said in interviews. "We chose not to. The mine foreman who buys the cheapest brake is not the one who has to stand at the top of the shaft when something goes wrong. We decided to make the brake we would want to use if we were still working underground."

The Aspiration That Still Drives Us

Twenty-five years on, Kapabrake has grown considerably. Five hundred systems installed. Twelve countries. A product range that covers mining, marine, lifting, oil and gas, and heavy industry. An engineering team of forty-plus people. ISO 9001 certification. International contracts.

But the aspiration remains exactly what it was in that garage in Krugersdorp: to build brake lining that don't fail. Not brakes that rarely fail. Not brakes that fail gracefully. Brakes that simply do not fail in normal operation, and that fail safely and predictably in abnormal conditions.

Every engineer hired at Kapabrake is told this story. Every design review begins with the same question the founders asked in the mine: what happens when this fails, and is that acceptable?

The answer, at Kapabrake, is always the same: make sure it isn't.
