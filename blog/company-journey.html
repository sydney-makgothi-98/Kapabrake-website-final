From Workshop to Mine Site: 25 Years of Building Trust Underground

By the Kapabrake Team February 2024 9 min read

In 1999, Kapabrake occupied a rented workshop space in the Johannesburg industrial belt. The equipment consisted of a few machining centres, borrowed test rigs, and five people who genuinely believed they could build a better brake. By most measures, it wasn't an obvious bet.

Twenty-five years later, those five people have become more than forty. The workshop has grown into a proper engineering and manufacturing facility. And the brakes — five hundred of them — are installed in mines, on vessels, in processing plants, and on oil platforms across twelve countries.

This is the story of how that happened. Not the sanitised version — the actual version, with the setbacks included.

The First Three Years: Learning What Customers Actually Need

The founding team made a decision in the early days that proved critical: they would not sell from a catalogue. Every enquiry that came in — however small — would be responded to with an engineering consultation, not a price list.

This was expensive. It was slow. The sales team (which was, at that point, one of the founders) spent hours on site visits that sometimes produced no revenue. But the intelligence gathered was invaluable. They learned what mine engineers actually needed — not what brake suppliers assumed they needed.

The first major contract came in 2001, eighteen months after founding. A medium-sized platinum mine in Rustenburg needed a replacement brake system for their No. 3 shaft hoist — an elderly imported system that had become increasingly unreliable and for which spares were no longer readily available. Kapabrake quoted a new system, manufactured locally, with local spares supply and local service support.

They got the contract. And then they had four months to deliver a system they had never built at that scale before.

"Those four months were the most intense period of our company's existence. We worked around the clock. We made mistakes. We fixed the mistakes. We delivered on time. And when that system passed commissioning, we had something we hadn't had before: proof that we could do this."

2003–2008: The ISO Years and Building Credibility

By 2003, Kapabrake had completed a handful of contracts and had a small but growing reputation in the Gauteng mining community. But the company was constrained by a credibility problem that every young engineering business faces: without ISO 9001 certification, the larger mining houses and multinationals wouldn't even look at them.

The decision was made to pursue certification seriously — not as a box-ticking exercise, but as a genuine process improvement programme. It took two years. Every engineering process was documented, reviewed, and in many cases redesigned. The manufacturing floor was reorganised. Quality control procedures were formalised.

When the certificate arrived in 2005, it was not just a piece of paper. It represented a genuinely different organisation — one with documented, repeatable processes that could scale.

The years between 2005 and 2008 saw the first significant growth in team size and contract volume. A major coal mining group awarded Kapabrake a framework contract covering brake inspections and replacements across their entire Mpumalanga coal portfolio. Suddenly, the company needed not just more engineers but a structured service team that could operate across multiple sites simultaneously.

The 2012 Pivot: Beyond Mining

The decision to expand beyond mining came from an unexpected source: a phone call from a port equipment maintenance manager in Durban who had heard about Kapabrake's mining work and wanted to know if they could do something similar for ship-to-shore cranes.

The honest answer was: we don't know, but let's find out.

A two-year process followed — studying marine standards, developing salt-resistant material specifications, engaging with classification societies, and ultimately producing a brake system that could withstand port environments. The Durban port contract was completed in 2012, and it opened a door into the marine and materials handling world that Kapabrake had not previously considered.

Oil and gas followed. The ATEX certification process — obtaining approval to operate in explosive atmospheres — was one of the most technically demanding challenges Kapabrake had ever undertaken. It required eighteen months of testing, third-party verification, and documentation that filled multiple filing cabinets. But when it was done, Kapabrake could legally supply braking equipment to petrochemical facilities and offshore platforms that competitors without certification simply couldn't access.

The 500th System and What It Meant

In 2018, Kapabrake installed its 500th brake system. The milestone was marked internally, though not publicly — the team felt it was more important to get the 501st system right than to celebrate the 500th.

But privately, the milestone meant something significant. It meant that five hundred installations — in mines, on ships, in oil plants, in factories — had been completed. That five hundred sets of engineers, operators, and maintenance teams had trusted Kapabrake with equipment that mattered. And that, as far as anyone knew, not one of those five hundred systems had ever been involved in a serious safety incident attributable to brake failure.

That record is not luck. It is the result of the engineering principles established in that garage in Krugersdorp, maintained through every design review and every factory test since 1999.

Where We Are Now — and Where We're Headed

Twenty-five years in, Kapabrake is a very different organisation from the one that delivered its first platinum mine hoist brake on a tight timeline and a shoestring budget. But the core question — what happens when this fails, and is that acceptable? — has never changed.

The next chapter is beginning to take shape. IoT-enabled condition monitoring. Predictive maintenance algorithms that can detect brake degradation before performance is affected. Smart systems that can communicate their own health status to plant control rooms without human inspection.

The technology is new. The mission is not.
