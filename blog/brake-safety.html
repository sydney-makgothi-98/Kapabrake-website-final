The Science of Safety: How We Test Industrial Brake Lining

By Kapabrake Engineering Team March 2024 11 min read

What does it actually mean to call a brake system "safe"? For a consumer product, safety might mean it meets a minimum regulatory standard. For an industrial braking system deployed on a mine hoist or an offshore crane, safety means something fundamentally different — it means the system will perform to specification in every conceivable operating scenario, including the ones that weren't anticipated.

This article walks through Kapabrake's testing methodology. It is not short, because testing — done properly — is not short. But for engineers specifying brake lining for critical applications, this is the kind of detail that matters.

Why Our Standards Exceed Regulatory Requirements

Regulatory bodies set minimum standards. They define the floor, not the ceiling. At Kapabrake, we have always tested to performance levels that exceed applicable standards, for a simple reason: regulations describe what the industry has collectively decided is acceptable based on historical data. But historical data is, by definition, backward-looking. It reflects failures that have already happened, not failures that haven't happened yet.

Our testing philosophy is forward-looking: we ask what could go wrong that hasn't gone wrong yet, and we test for it anyway. This approach is more expensive and more time-consuming. It is also, in our view, the only intellectually honest way to certify a safety-critical system.

Phase 1: Design Verification Testing

Before a new design reaches manufacturing, it undergoes design verification testing (DVT) — a systematic process that validates that the design meets its specification under controlled conditions.

Static Load Testing

Every brake design is tested for static holding capability at 150% of rated load. A hoist brake rated for 20 tonnes must hold 30 tonnes without movement or creep for a minimum of 15 minutes. This margin exists because real-world loads are not always what operators intend — shock loads, inertia effects, and mechanical advantage variations can all exceed nominal ratings momentarily.

Dynamic Stop Testing

Dynamic stopping — arresting a moving load — is more complex than static holding. The energy absorbed during a stop is a function of both load and velocity, and it must be dissipated as heat through the brake disc and pad without causing thermal failure. Our dynamic test programme covers:

  • Stop testing at rated load and rated speed
  • Stop testing at 125% rated load and rated speed
  • Emergency stop testing at rated load and 150% rated speed
  • Repeated stops in quick succession (simulating high-frequency duty cycles)
  • Stops from maximum speed with minimum time between applications

Thermal Cycle Testing

Heat is the primary failure mode for brake friction materials. Our thermal test programme subjects prototype systems to rapid sequential stops — typically 50 to 100 in quick succession — while monitoring disc temperature, pad temperature, and friction coefficient. We establish the system's thermal envelope and verify that performance degradation under heating is within acceptable bounds, and that performance recovers fully on cooling.

"A brake that works well cold but fades after ten stops is not a safe brake. We need to know exactly how the system performs from the first stop to the hundredth — and that the hundredth stop is as reliable as the first."

Phase 2: Environmental Qualification Testing

Design verification proves that a brake works in ideal conditions. Environmental qualification proves that it works in the conditions where it will actually be used.

Temperature Extremes

Our standard temperature qualification range is -20°C to +80°C for general industrial applications. Mining and oil and gas variants are tested to -40°C and +120°C ambient. At low temperatures, we test that hydraulic actuators operate without sluggishness and that seals maintain integrity without brittleness. At high temperatures, we verify that friction materials maintain their coefficient and that seal compounds don't extrude or degrade.

Dust and Moisture Ingress Testing

IP ingress protection ratings are not just labels — they require verified testing. Our standard mine brake assemblies are tested to IP65 (dust-tight, water jet resistant) as a minimum. The test procedure involves exposing assembled brake units to coal dust at concentrations that match typical mine environments, followed by washing with a directed water jet. Post-test, the units are disassembled and inspected for ingress.

Salt Spray Testing (Marine Applications)

All marine-specification brakes undergo 500-hour continuous salt spray testing in accordance with ISO 9227. This is more stringent than the 168-hour standard. After testing, brakes are disassembled, inspected for corrosion at all critical interfaces, and functionally tested to verify that no corrosion has affected performance or safety.

Vibration and Shock Testing

Industrial machinery generates vibration. So do the vehicles that transport brake lining to site. Our vibration test programme covers both sinusoidal sweep testing (to identify resonance frequencies) and random vibration testing that simulates real transport and operational profiles. Shock testing verifies that the design survives the impulse loads it will experience during equipment operation and during handling.

Phase 3: Fatigue and Life Testing

Safety-critical components must be tested for fatigue life — the accumulation of damage under repeated loading cycles that eventually leads to failure. A brake system that passes all short-term tests but fails after five years of operation is not acceptable.

Actuator Fatigue Testing

Hydraulic and pneumatic actuators — the components that apply and release the brake — undergo fatigue testing at 200% of the intended number of operating cycles. A system specified for 100,000 cycles per year over a 20-year design life undergoes 4,000,000 test cycles, with inspection intervals to track degradation modes.

Disc and Pad Wear Testing

We establish pad wear rates empirically — not from suppliers' data sheets. Brake pads are run on test rigs at representative duty cycles until they reach minimum thickness, and the total number of stops and total energy absorbed is recorded. This data is used to establish maintenance intervals that include a safety margin — we never let pads run to minimum in service.

Phase 4: Failure Mode and Effect Testing

The final and most important phase of our testing programme is FMET — failure mode and effect testing. For every identified failure mode (hydraulic pressure loss, actuator seizure, pad material separation, fastener failure, corrosion-induced binding), we deliberately induce the failure and observe and measure the system's response.

The question we are asking is not whether the system will fail — everything eventually fails. The question is whether, when it fails, it fails in a way that is acceptable. A brake that fails to release when it should is a production problem. A brake that releases when it should hold is a safety catastrophe. The design must ensure that the latter is impossible — or, where that cannot be guaranteed, that redundant systems prevent it from being consequential.

This is why Kapabrake hoist brakes use spring-applied, hydraulically-released actuation: the spring force ensures engagement on any loss of hydraulic pressure. Fail-safe, not fail-dangerous.

What We Ship After Testing

Every system that leaves our facility ships with a test record — a complete documented history of the tests performed on that specific unit, the results achieved, and the signature of the engineer who verified those results. This is not a standard practice in the industry. We believe it should be.

Independent Product Verification

Kapabrake lining is independently tested to SAE J661 in Detroit, USA. Our KB2 lining achieved a G/F rating and KB3 was rated Class H, confirming that our material performs within the expected friction and thermal stability windows required for critical braking applications.

All batches are also validated on our own in-house test rigs, where we measure stopping times, heat build-up, wear rate, bonding strength, and flexibility after extreme heat exposure. This dual verification process gives our customers confidence that both the material and the assembled system meet the highest standards.

When an engineer on a mine site receives a Kapabrake system, they receive the engineering calculations, the test data, the maintenance schedule, and the contact details of the Kapabrake engineer responsible for that product line. Not a catalogue. Not a hotline that goes to a call centre.

Safety is not a certification. It is a decision, made repeatedly, at every stage of design and manufacturing, to prioritise performance over convenience and reliability over cost. It is an expensive decision to make. It is a far more expensive decision not to make.
